Verses Where Jesus Is Called a Prophet and a Man

November 30, 2025 • 6 min read • By Lorem Ipsum

The New Testament repeatedly records people speaking about Jesus as a prophet and as a man. These descriptions reflect how ordinary people, crowds, and even some of his disciples understood him during his earthly ministry. While Christian theology later develops doctrines about his divinity, these verses show the way he was recognized in real time: as a messenger of God, powerful in word and deed, and as a human being who walked, spoke, and lived among his people.

1. Jesus Recognized as a Prophet – Witness from Matthew

In the Gospel of Matthew, the crowds surrounding Jesus clearly identify him in prophetic terms. They do not call him “God” or “a divine being,” but “the prophet from Nazareth,” placing him in continuity with the prophets of the Old Testament.

Matthew 21:11
“And the crowds answered, ‘This is Jesus, the prophet from Nazareth in Galilee.’”

Here the people give Jesus a very specific title: the prophet. He is known by name, by hometown, and by role. This is a public confession from the crowd, not a private or hidden belief.

Matthew 21:46
“They looked for a way to arrest him, but they were afraid of the crowd because the people held that he was a prophet.”

The leaders wanted to arrest Jesus, but the people’s conviction that he was a prophet protected him, at least for a time. Again, Jesus is viewed as a prophet – a servant of God – not as God Himself.

2. “One of the Prophets” – Testimonies from Mark and Luke

Other Gospels add more voices from the crowds, showing that many people placed Jesus among the ranks of earlier prophets like Elijah and John the Baptist. They recognise a holy man sent by God, continuing the prophetic tradition.

Mark 6:15
“Others said, ‘He is Elijah.’ And still others claimed, ‘He is a prophet, like one of the prophets of long ago.’”

Here Jesus is compared to Elijah and to the prophets “of long ago.” This language connects him to familiar prophetic figures – men chosen by God, but still human servants of God.

Mark 8:28
“They replied, ‘Some say John the Baptist; others say Elijah; and still others, one of the prophets.’”

When Jesus asks what people are saying about him, the answers are all within the category of prophet. The options people give – John the Baptist, Elijah, or “one of the prophets” – show that his mission is understood as prophetic, not as God walking among them.

Luke 7:16
“They were all filled with awe and praised God. ‘A great prophet has appeared among us,’ they said. ‘God has come to help his people.’”

After witnessing one of Jesus’ miracles, the people glorify God and say, “A great prophet has appeared among us.” Jesus’ miracle leads them to praise God above, while identifying Jesus himself as a great prophet sent by that same God.

Luke 24:19 (disciples on the road to Emmaus)
“He was a prophet, powerful in word and deed before God and all the people.”

Even after the crucifixion, the disciples on the road to Emmaus describe Jesus in the past tense as “a prophet, powerful in word and deed.” Again, his status as a prophet, acting before God, is emphasised.

· · ·
3. “Surely This Is the Prophet” – Prophetic Titles in John

The Gospel of John preserves several powerful statements where different people recognise Jesus explicitly as a prophet. These confessions come from a Samaritan woman, crowds who witnessed miracles, and a man healed from blindness.

John 4:19 (Samaritan woman)
“Sir,” the woman said, “I can see that you are a prophet.”

The Samaritan woman, after her conversation with Jesus and his knowledge of her life, moves naturally to the conclusion that he is “a prophet” – someone who receives knowledge from God.

John 6:14
“After the people saw the sign Jesus performed, they began to say, ‘Surely this is the Prophet who is to come into the world.’”

Many Jews were expecting “the Prophet” foretold in the scriptures. After witnessing a miracle, the crowd identifies Jesus with this figure – a promised prophet, not a member of the Trinity.

John 7:40
“On hearing his words, some of the people said, ‘Surely this man is the Prophet.’”

Once again, Jesus is called “the Prophet.” The phrase “this man” keeps the focus on his visible, human presence, while the title “Prophet” marks his role as God’s spokesman.

John 9:17 (the man born blind, after being healed)
“He is a prophet.”

The man who was healed reaches his own conclusion about Jesus: “He is a prophet.” Jesus’ miracle leads him to recognise Jesus as a man sent by God, not God Himself.

· · ·
4. Jesus Described as a Man – Human Language in John and Scholarly Reflections

Alongside the prophetic titles, the New Testament also uses very clear language about Jesus as a man. These verses highlight his humanity. In Christian theology this does not necessarily deny claims of divinity, but they show how people around him understood him: as a human being who spoke the truth he heard from God.

John 7:27
“But we know where this man is from; when the Christ comes, no one will know where he is from.”

The people call Jesus “this man” and speak about his known origin. They contrast their knowledge of his background with their expectations about the Christ, again emphasising his visible humanity.

John 7:46 (officers sent to arrest Jesus)
“No one ever spoke the way this man does.”

Even those sent to arrest Jesus are impressed by him and refer to him simply as “this man.” His speech is unique and powerful, yet he is still described with normal human language.

John 8:40
“But now you are trying to kill me, a man who has told you the truth that I heard from God.”

Here Jesus speaks about himself directly: “a man who has told you the truth that I heard from God.” He clearly distinguishes himself from God by presenting himself as the human messenger who conveys God’s truth.

John 9:11
“The man they call Jesus made some mud and put it on my eyes…”

The healed man again uses ordinary human language: “the man they call Jesus.” The miracle is great, but the one performing it is still described as “the man” Jesus.

John 10:33
“We are not stoning you for any good work,” they replied, “but for blasphemy, because you, a mere man, claim to be God.”

Jesus’ opponents accuse him of blasphemy precisely because they see him as “a mere man” who is making exalted claims. Their words show clearly how they perceived him: human, not divine.

These human and prophetic descriptions have led some modern Christian scholars to re-examine the so-called “deification texts” – the passages where Jesus appears to be called “God.” For example, the New Testament scholar James Dunn observes that when each piece of evidence is examined closely, serious textual and translational questions arise. He notes that in almost all of the places in the New Testament where Jesus seems to be directly called “God,” there are problems: either issues in the manuscript tradition, or difficulties in how the text should be translated.

According to this kind of analysis, many of the strongest “divinity texts” are not as straightforward as they may first appear. Dunn points out that this way of explicitly calling Jesus “God” does not occur in the first three Gospels (Matthew, Mark, and Luke) at all. Instead, those Gospels consistently present him as God’s servant, a prophet, and a man empowered by God – which harmonises with the verses listed above, where Jesus is repeatedly recognised as a prophet and as a human being.
